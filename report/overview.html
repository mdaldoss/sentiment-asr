Speech Sentiment Analyzer

What the field can do, what we built, what we found, and what would make it better. — index · full results dashboard

The one question

A model that classifies emotion from speech can cheat. Given the audio of someone saying "that's just wonderful" in a flat, exhausted voice, it can transcribe the words, read their sentiment, and answer positive — without ever having listened to the delivery. On most benchmarks that cheat scores well, because in most recordings the words and the tone agree.

It stops working the moment they don't. And for a voice companion for seniors — no screen, no camera, no face to read — tone is the only channel the words don't already cover. So this project is built less to maximise a score and more to measure whether the model hears prosody at all, using speech where the words say one thing and the delivery says another.

State of the art — what the field can actually do

Everything in this section is other researchers' published results, not ours. Ours start in the next section.

SettingBest reportedWhat it means
Acted emotion, scripted (IEMOCAP)~0.78 UAR Actors performing emotions on cue. The easiest realistic setting, and the one most papers report.
Naturalistic speech (MSP-Podcast)~0.65 UAR Real, spontaneous speech. A large replication study found this is roughly where the field tops out.

Three findings from the recent literature matter more than the leaderboard, because they shape what is worth building:

1. Progress has been smaller than it looks. A 2025 replication study charting 15 years of deep learning for speech emotion recognition found very low correlation between a model's performance and its publication year, parameter count, or compute. Many modern models landed at or below the 2009 challenge winners. The authors conclude that hyperparameter tuning — not architectural innovation — may account for much of the reported gains. Their top models agreed with each other on only 55–80% of predictions, meaning they are learning different solutions rather than converging on the truth. (arXiv:2508.02448)

2. The "reads words instead of tone" failure is documented, not hypothetical. Work evaluating spoken-language models on deliberately emotionally incongruent speech shows models follow the transcript rather than the delivery (arXiv:2510.25054), and a 2026 study on acoustic-cue alignment in audio language models asks the same question directly (arXiv:2606.07309). This is the exact failure mode this project is instrumented to catch.

3. The field audits itself and doesn't like what it sees. A 2026 paper on the gap between motivation and practice finds that speech-emotion research is routinely justified by healthcare and elderly-care applications, yet rarely evaluated on those populations — predominantly using acted datasets of young adults. It also finds that many papers skip speaker-independent evaluation, and that significance testing and confidence intervals are frequently absent. (arXiv:2604.25776)

Where the frontier is moving: general-purpose audio language models (Qwen2-Audio, SALMONN and similar) now perform emotion recognition alongside transcription and sound classification, and 2026 benchmarks such as VoxEmo (arXiv:2603.08936) exist specifically to test them. Reinforcement-learning approaches to generalised emotion recognition (arXiv:2509.15654) are an active line. This project evaluates none of them — see improvement #13.

What is realistically possible

The honest summary: emotion-from-tone works well enough to demonstrate convincingly on acted benchmark speech, and degrades sharply on a real speaker the model has never heard — in the worst case to near chance. Any claim that this is "solved" is a claim about the benchmark, not about a product. That is not a flaw in this implementation; per the replication study above, it is roughly where the whole field sits.

What we built

Three systems spanning the words ↔ tone axis, behind one interface, so the evaluation harness scores them identically and the comparison is apples-to-apples:

SolutionWhat it can possibly knowLicence
ALexical — speech recognition → text sentiment Only the words. The deliberate control: it cannot hear tone.MIT
BAcoustic — frozen speech encoder + small trained head Only the tone. Never sees a transcript. two backends: MIT, or CC‑BY‑NC‑SA (research only)
CFusion — calibrated combination of A and B, can abstain Both.inherits

Having A in the design is what makes the whole thing measurable: A is a model that provably only reads words, so its score on tone-vs-words tests is the "pure cheating" reference line that B and C are compared against.

Everything converts to a shared valence / arousal / dominance reading; sentiment is a threshold on valence. That keeps the output interpretable (you can see why, not just the label) and gives arousal for free, which the voice-health roadmap needs.

Two acoustic backends, deliberately: permissive is a general-purpose speech encoder (WavLM, MIT) with our own small classifier trained on top; research is a model already fine-tuned end-to-end for emotion on naturalistic speech (audeering/wav2vec2), which is stronger but non-commercial. Keeping both turns a licensing question into a measured trade-off instead of an assertion.

Results

1. On the benchmark, tone beats words decisively

CREMA-D, 300 clips, speakers held out of training. PSI = of the clips where words and tone disagree, the fraction where the model followed the tone. 1.0 = listens; 0.0 = reads the transcript.

SolutionUARMacro-F1PSI
A — Lexical (words only)0.360 0.2640.090
B — Acoustic (tone only)0.797 0.7960.920
C — Fusion (both)0.797 0.7960.891

Exactly the shape the design predicts. The lexical model sits near chance and has a PSI of 0.09 — it follows the words almost every time, because that is all it has. The acoustic model reaches PSI 0.92: it is genuinely listening.

2. Sloppy splitting would have inflated this

Same model, same data, only the split changed — speakers allowed to appear in both training and test:

SplitnSpeakers in both sidesUAR
Speaker-disjoint (honest)14700 0.744
Random (leaky)1488 91 0.761

A free +0.017 UAR for doing the evaluation wrong. Published as a warning, not hidden.

3. On a real, unseen voice, it mostly falls apart

27 clips per take, same speaker, same prompts, recorded twice on separate occasions. Chance = 0.333.

SolutionTake 1Take 2Self-consistency (r)
A — Lexical0.333 0.370—
B — Acoustic, permissive (MIT)0.444 0.519 -0.19
B — Acoustic, research (non-commercial)0.444 0.593 0.92

The most important number in this project is that last column. It asks: given two recordings of the same person saying the same sentence the same way, does the model give the same reading? The research backend does (r = 0.92). The shipping-licensed permissive backend does not (r = -0.19) — its readings are essentially uncorrelated with themselves. A model can only be as accurate as it is repeatable, and on this voice that one is not repeatable at all. Accuracy tables alone would never have revealed this.

Note the reversal: on the benchmark the permissive backend scores 0.80 against the research backend's 0.45; on a real voice they swap places. The benchmark number is a home-turf advantage — the permissive probe was trained on that exact corpus.

4. The sharpest result: "acoustic" does not always mean prosodic

E5 is 90 synthetic clips in which 60 have the words and the delivery deliberately disagreeing — the condition the whole project is built to measure, finally available at scale because one TTS vendor can set delivery independently of the text. Here PSI is the metric, not UAR: the gold label is the delivery, so a model reading words scores near zero by construction.

SolutionUARPSIWhat it follows
A — Lexical (words only)0.3330.000the words, every time
B — Acoustic, permissive0.5110.682mostly the tone
B — Acoustic, research0.4000.211mostly the words
C — Fusion0.4890.605between, as designed

UAR chance 0.333; PSI chance 0.5. Solution A scoring exactly 0.000 is the floor behaving perfectly — it is the proof that the labels are right and the contradiction is real.

The finding: the two acoustic backends heard identical audio and went opposite ways. The permissive one followed the delivery 68% of the time; the research one followed the words 79% of the time, below chance, despite never receiving a transcript. That is an empirical confirmation of a caveat the audeering authors published themselves — their model's valence performance draws partly on implicit linguistic information learned during fine-tuning. The "acoustic" model has substantially learned to read words out of audio, and only a set built to contradict itself could expose it.

This also complicates the backend choice rather than settling it. On a real voice (E3) the research backend is more accurate and far more self-consistent; on prosody-versus-words it is much worse. Neither is simply better — which one to pick depends on whether the product needs a stable reading or one that is genuinely about delivery.

5. Synthetic emotional speech: one vendor failed, another worked

Building test data by asking a text-to-speech vendor for "say this sadly" seemed cheap. It wasn't: with the first vendor, a classifier could recover the requested emotion from the generated audio at 0.175 against 0.200 chance — the emotion simply wasn't in the audio. Rather than accept that as a general fact, the same 40-clip design was re-run on a second vendor whose API controls delivery independently of the words: 0.500 with that control on, 0.200 with it off. So the finding is "that vendor, on this content" — not "synthetic emotional speech is impossible."

E6 — eight real speakers, and what the benchmark numbers do not survive

Every human result above this point rests on one speaker recorded twice; E5's incongruence is synthetic. E6 is 160 clips from 8 speakers, 111 of them incongruent — laptop-microphone audio from mostly non-native English speakers, which is a far harder condition than CREMA-D's acted studio recordings. It is the first real cross-speaker evaluation this project has had, and the first human multi-speaker set on which PSI means anything.

Everything below is zero-shot: fitted on CREMA-D, never having heard these voices. Intervals are a 95% percentile bootstrap over clips. Chance is 0.333 for UAR, 0.500 for PSI.

This is the most important result in the project, and it is a negative one. Of five systems, one has a UAR interval clearing chance, and it clears it by 0.003. The acoustic backend that reaches 0.797 on the CREMA-D subset lands in the 0.33–0.46 range here. Nothing fitted on CREMA-D transfers to real multi-speaker audio, and the earlier one-speaker and synthetic transfer numbers were flattering.

Two things survive. The lexical floor holds: a transcript-only model follows the transcript, with its whole PSI interval below chance — which also checks the one column of E6 that was labelled by hand. And the research backend follows the words too, from audio alone with no transcript in its path, its entire interval below chance. E5 measured that on two synthetic voices; E6 replicates it on eight real ones with an interval behind it. Calling a model “acoustic” does not make it prosodic.

Not claimed: that the permissive backend is prosody-sensitive. Its own PSI interval contains 0.5. The direction is consistent across E5 and E6, but only the research backend's failure is statistically clean — on an earlier six-speaker build this looked like a clear win for the permissive backend, and the intervals on the full set say the two merely touch.

A claim withdrawn: on that six-speaker build, training on E6's clips alone beat CREMA-D 0.452 to 0.278, and it nearly went into this report as evidence that matched data beats more data. On eight speakers the same configuration gives 0.222 and the effect reversed. It was noise on a 20-clip test split, and the bootstrap interval is the only reason it was not written up as a finding.

What didn't work

Kept visible, because a take-home that reports only successes is reporting selectively:

  • The first TTS vendor's emotion tags — not audibly rendered; a control on human speech proved our instruments were fine, so the audio was the problem.
  • The synthetic incongruence set built on it — 76 clips, inexpressive, kept as the exhibit rather than deleted or quietly completed.
  • The permissive backend on a new voice — near chance, never predicted "positive" at all on one take, and not self-consistent across takes.
  • Per-speaker recalibration — fitting personal thresholds helped in 1 of 4 backend×take combinations and hurt in 3. Reported as a negative result rather than shipped.
  • Adding more training data — folding our own recordings and synthetic clips into training moved benchmark UAR by at most 0.003. At ~1% of training volume, too little to matter.
  • The live browser demo — planned, not built (see #9).

Areas for improvement

Ordered by value per hour of work, not by ambition. The first four are cheap and materially change how much the rest can be trusted.

Tier 1 — credibility fixes ~1 day total

  1. The full pipeline command is broken. make all calls make eval, which invokes a module entry point that does not exist (ssa/eval/__main__.py). The project's own definition of "done" is that make all works end to end — so the first command a reviewer types fails. The individual evaluation scripts all work; only the top-level entry point is missing. Highest priority: it costs an hour and it is the first impression.
  2. No confidence intervals anywhere. Key comparisons rest on 27–54 clips. The difference between 0.519 and 0.593 on n=27 is very likely noise, and nothing in the report currently says so. Bootstrap confidence intervals on UAR and PSI would either harden or retract several claims — and the field's own 2026 self-critique names missing significance testing as a systemic failure. This is the one methodological failure mode from that critique that this project currently shares.
  3. The headline number is inconsistent. Benchmark UAR appears as 0.797 (300-clip stratified subset) in some places and 0.744 (full 1,470-clip test set) in others. Both are honest; reporting both without a stated default invites the reader to assume the flattering one is the real one. Pick the full test set as canonical.
  4. The research backend is missing from the main results table. Its benchmark score exists only inside the backend-comparison file, so the headline comparison silently omits the backend that turned out to be better on real speech.

Tier 2 — evidence gaps days to weeks

  1. No usable incongruence set at scale. Addressed by E5: 90 clips on a vendor whose delivery control works independently of the transcript, 60 of them contradictory — and it produced the sharpest result in the project (see below). The older 76-clip Cartesia set is still unevaluated; it is now a historical exhibit rather than a gap, since E5 supersedes what it was for.
  2. Everything about real-world performance rests on one speaker. One person, two takes, 27 clips each. That is enough to demonstrate a transfer failure exists; it is not enough to estimate how large it is. Ten speakers would change this from an anecdote into a measurement.
  3. No cross-corpus result. Train on one acted corpus, test on a different one — the standard generalisation check — was planned and never run. Two suitable public datasets were identified and are freely available; neither was fetched. This is probably the single highest-value missing experiment.
  4. Zero elderly voices. The product is for seniors. Age changes the voice in ways that can read as sadness to a model trained on younger speakers — the project argues this from clinical literature and explicitly labels it as unmeasured. It remains the largest untested assumption, and it is precisely the motivation-vs-evaluation gap the 2026 field critique describes.

Tier 3 — making it a convincing demonstration mostly done

  1. You cannot speak into it. Built (make demo-web): record in the browser, get both acoustic backends and the lexical control scored on your own voice. Captures raw PCM via the Web Audio API and routes it through the same resample/normalise path every evaluated clip takes, so the demo cannot flatter the model with preprocessing evaluation never got. It opens already showing a committed clip — positive words spoken flatly — rather than an empty shell.
  2. Show the valence/arousal plane. Built: the demo plots where a clip lands on valence×arousal with the quadrants named, so arousal stops being computed and discarded.
  3. Show both backends disagreeing, live. Built: both are scored on every clip, side by side. On the bundled example they disagree immediately.
  4. Only three coarse classes are ever reported. The benchmark has six emotions; everything is collapsed to positive/neutral/negative before scoring. A six-way confusion matrix would show whether the model confuses anger with sadness or merely smooths everything toward neutral — much more informative about understanding. Still open.

Tier 4 — modelling research

  1. No audio language model was tried. The current frontier — Qwen2-Audio, SALMONN and similar — performs emotion recognition directly and is entirely absent from the comparison. Even a zero-shot baseline would establish whether this project's approach is competitive or obsolete.
  2. The encoder was used in exactly one configuration. A single pooled representation feeding a linear classifier. The replication study cited above found that pooling across layers and hyperparameter choice affected results more than architecture did — suggesting cheap gains are available before anything exotic is needed.
  3. The self-consistency failure is unexplained. We know the permissive backend contradicts itself across two takes of the same voice. We do not know why — speaker sensitivity, pooling, recording-level differences, or the classifier. Diagnosing it is more valuable than another point of benchmark accuracy, because repeatability is a precondition for everything downstream.

Future development

DirectionWhy it mattersWhat it needs
Per-person baselining, over weeks A companion device has one persistent user. Learning that person's normal voice dissolves both the age confound and the "is this illness or just how they sound" problem. A short-horizon version was tested and failed — which is evidence for needing the long horizon, not against the idea. Longitudinal recordings from consenting users.
Train on naturalistic, not acted, speech Acted emotion is performed and exaggerated; the transfer failure measured here is exactly what that predicts. The field's naturalistic benchmark tops out around 0.65 UAR — a more honest target to build against. MSP-Podcast access.
An elderly-voice pilot Moves the project's central assumption from "argued from clinical literature" to "measured" — and closes the exact gap the 2026 field critique identifies. A small consented recording study.
Audio-LLM comparison Establishes whether a purpose-built small pipeline still beats a general foundation model on this task, or whether the sensible architecture is now a prompt. Inference budget; a careful incongruence test so the LLM can't cheat via transcript.
Distress detection, not just sentiment Arousal is already computed and thrown away. Negative-and-high-arousal (agitation) versus negative-and-low-arousal (withdrawal) is a more actionable signal for a care companion than "negative". Labels for the quadrants; clinical input on what should trigger what.
Resolve the licence question The backend that works better on real voices cannot ship commercially. Either licence it, or close the gap with the permissive one — currently an open business decision disguised as a technical one. A vendor conversation, or improvement #15.
